## What changed

When generative AI arrived, the Information Experience (IX) organization at Splunk had to scale content without adding headcount. I founded the IX AI Council to do that safely. It set tool policy and taught the writers to use the tools, working between engineering, product, and content.

## Where we started

Writers were trying unapproved tools on their own, which was a security risk, and many of them feared AI would replace them. We also had a mandate to use AI with no standard for how to use it on documentation.

## Approved tools and a data policy

We moved from everyone picking their own tool to an approved set. I audited enterprise tools against our security and style requirements, and the council published a Red, Yellow, Green policy for which data could go where. One rule did most of the work: no proprietary code in public LLMs.

## Prompts kept like code

I set up a shared library of system prompts, so writers could review and reuse prompts the way engineers handle code. That gave 60+ people the same starting point and made the tone of AI drafts consistent across the team.

## The review workflow

In the workflow, AI drafts and a writer edits. Raw information goes to an AI agent, which produces a draft. A writer reviews it. A rejected draft goes back to the agent for another pass. An approved one gets a style and tone polish and is published to the docs portal.

Nothing reaches the portal without a person approving it. That was also my answer to the writers who feared AI would replace them.

## What the writers could do

Writers drafted with approved AI tools inside the publishing pipeline, and they knew which data could go into which tool.
